# Part Month Salary Calculator — Starting or Leaving Mid-Month

> There are two accepted methods and they give different answers, which is why your first payslip may not match what you expected.

Reference notes for the UK tax year **2026/27**, computed from HMRC's
published rates rather than copied from another site.

## How it works

**Calendar-day basis.** Annual salary ÷ 365 × days employed in the month. Every day counts, weekends included.

**Working-day basis.** Monthly salary ÷ working days in that month × days worked.

A worked case. You join on **Tuesday 15 September 2026** on £36,000. September has 30 calendar days and 22 working days; you are employed for 16 calendar days, of which 12 are working days.

| Basis | Sum | Pay |
|---|---|---:|
| Calendar day | £36,000 ÷ 365 × 16 | £1,578.08 |
| Working day | £3,000.00 ÷ 22 × 12 | £1,636.36 |

A difference of £58.28 on one payslip. Neither is wrong; your contract or your employer's payroll policy decides which applies.

## What this does not cover

Some employers use 260 working days a year rather than the actual days in the month, which gives a third answer again. Check your contract before disputing a figure.

## Source and tax year

Figures are for the 2026/27 tax year, which runs 6 April 2026
to 5 April 2027, and were checked against
[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-and-thresholds-for-employers-2026-to-2027) on 28 August 2026.

Rates change every April, and often at a Budget. Check the tax year a figure
belongs to before relying on it.
